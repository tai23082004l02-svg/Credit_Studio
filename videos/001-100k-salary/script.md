# Why You Feel Broke on a $100,000 Salary

Chỉ đưa phần tiếng Anh bên dưới vào công cụ đọc. Không đọc tên đoạn hoặc phần hướng dẫn tiếng Việt. Giọng Mỹ tự nhiên, khoảng 140–150 từ/phút; nghỉ nhẹ giữa các đoạn. Thời lượng thực tế phụ thuộc giọng đọc.

## 01 — Hook

You finally did it.

You got the job. You got the raise. Your salary now has six figures.

One hundred thousand dollars a year.

That sounds like the moment money is supposed to stop being stressful.

But somehow, you're still checking your bank account before ordering takeout.

Your car needs new tires, and suddenly you're doing mental gymnastics.

And payday?

Payday still feels like a rescue mission.

So what happened?

How can someone make a hundred thousand dollars a year and still feel broke?

Let's follow the money. Because the answer starts before you buy a single thing.

## 02 — Take-home pay

Meet Alex.

Alex makes one hundred thousand dollars a year.

Divide that by twelve, and you get about eight thousand three hundred dollars a month.

Sounds pretty comfortable.

Except that isn't what lands in Alex's bank account.

Taxes take a portion. Health insurance takes another. Retirement contributions may come out before the paycheck arrives, too.

For our example, let's say Alex brings home six thousand dollars a month after those deductions.

That's an illustration, not a universal paycheck. The actual amount depends on things like location, benefits, and tax circumstances.

But here's the important part.

Alex hears “a hundred thousand dollars.”

Alex's checking account sees “six thousand a month.”

Those are two very different starting points for a budget.

And if Alex spends based on the bigger number, the smaller number is going to have a problem.

## 03 — Fixed costs

Now let's give that six thousand dollars somewhere to go.

Rent: twenty-three hundred dollars.

Not a mansion. Just an apartment in a fairly expensive area.

Transportation: eight hundred and fifty dollars.

That's the car payment, insurance, gas, and some money set aside for maintenance.

Groceries and eating out: six hundred and fifty.

Utilities, internet, and phone: four hundred.

Student loans or other debt payments: another four hundred.

Then three hundred dollars for things like subscriptions, clothes, and gifts.

Total?

Four thousand nine hundred dollars.

Alex has eleven hundred dollars left.

Now, eleven hundred dollars isn't nothing. This budget still has room.

But notice what happened.

Most of the paycheck was already committed before Alex made a single decision about this weekend.

The money didn't disappear.

It arrived with a waiting list.

## 04 — Irregular expenses

Then real life walks in.

A trip to the dentist.

A flight to a family wedding.

A laptop that decides today is its last day.

These expenses feel like surprises because they don't happen every month.

But some version of them happens most years.

If Alex needs an average of six hundred dollars a month to cover those irregular expenses, that eleven hundred dollars becomes five hundred.

Five hundred dollars of breathing room.

Enough to make progress, yes.

But a lot less than Alex imagined when signing that six-figure offer.

And if those irregular costs aren't planned for, they may land on a credit card instead.

Next month, the budget gets a new expense.

A payment for something that already happened.

That's how a decent income can start to feel like it's always one step behind.

## 05 — Lifestyle creep

But there's another part of this story.

A few years ago, Alex made less money.

The apartment was smaller. The car was older. Dinner delivery was an occasional treat.

Then the raises started coming.

And every upgrade had a reasonable explanation.

The nicer apartment cut the commute.

The newer car felt more reliable.

The extra subscriptions were only a few dollars each.

None of those decisions had to be a mistake.

The problem was that every raise came with a new commitment.

More money came in.

More money was promised away.

This is lifestyle creep.

It rarely feels like one dramatic shopping spree.

It feels like slowly turning yesterday's luxuries into today's normal expenses.

And once a higher standard of living becomes normal, stepping back can feel like losing something.

Even when stepping back would give you more freedom.

## 06 — Social comparison

Then Alex opens social media.

Someone just bought a house.

Someone else is in Italy.

A person Alex barely remembers from high school is posting from a boat.

Nobody is posting their insurance deductible.

Nobody is making a highlight reel of their credit card interest.

And a photo doesn't tell you whether a purchase was paid for with savings, debt, family help, or a much bigger income.

But your brain fills in the blanks anyway.

Everyone else seems comfortable.

So you start treating their spending as evidence of what you should be able to afford.

Now your paycheck has to support two lives.

The life you actually live.

And the life you feel like you should be living.

That's an expensive comparison.

## 07 — Action plan

So how does Alex get some breathing room?

Start with the number that actually arrives.

Build the plan around take-home pay, not the salary in the job offer.

Then look at the biggest recurring commitments.

Housing. Transportation. Debt payments.

Canceling a subscription can help.

But if the real pressure is a car that costs too much every month, a cheaper streaming plan won't solve the whole problem.

Some changes take time. A lease may need to end. A debt may need a repayment plan.

You don't have to fix everything this afternoon.

Next, stop calling predictable irregular expenses emergencies.

Car repairs, annual bills, and holiday spending can each get a small amount set aside every payday.

When the bill arrives, the money already has a job.

And when your income rises, decide what happens to the increase before it becomes part of your routine.

Some can improve your life today.

Some can build savings, reduce debt, or support longer-term goals.

The point isn't to reject every upgrade.

It's to keep every upgrade from consuming the entire raise.

## 08 — Ending

Of course, not every tight budget is a lifestyle problem.

Childcare, medical needs, supporting family, and living in an expensive area can put real pressure on a good income.

Sometimes the biggest issue isn't careless spending.

It's that the essential costs are genuinely high.

But whatever the cause, the first useful step is the same.

Find out where the money is committed.

Because a higher salary gives you more options.

It doesn't automatically give you a plan.

For Alex, financial progress might start with one less payment, a smaller fixed expense, or a cash buffer that finally survives until the next payday.

Not something impressive enough to post.

Just enough space to stop feeling trapped.

And that raises a better question than, “Do I make good money?”

How much of the money I make is helping me build the life I actually want?
